\begin{abstract}
Screening half-Heusler thermoelectrics needs the lattice thermal conductivity ($\kappa_L$) that a
laboratory will measure, yet first-principles calculations, and the machine-learning models trained
on them, predict the calculated value, and how far the two differ has not been measured across the
class. Using a curated corpus that keeps calculations and measurements apart, we find that published
calculations exceed the measured $\kappa_L$ of the same compound by a median factor of \num{1.69},
in almost every bonding family, and that the excess shrinks as temperature rises. A two-parameter
transfer function fitted to published calculations removes most of it and carries over to an
independent machine-learned force field. Paired with a machine-learning model that predicts the
calculation from crystal structure, and tested with each compound's chemistry withheld from every
fitted step, the route predicts measured $\kappa_L$ with a median error of \SI{33.1}{\percent}, with
\SI{87.2}{\percent} of compounds within a factor of two, inside a stated domain of applicability (18
valence electrons, cubic, no other polymorph). Its typical disagreement with measurement is similar
in size to the spread between two laboratories measuring the same compound. It beats baselines that
ignore the compound's identity, although only marginally when each chemistry cluster is counted
once, and gives \SI{37.4}{\percent} over all 50 measured compounds without the domain screens. We
issue ten predictions for unmeasured compounds, with uncertainty intervals whose coverage is
untested for the four that rest on a single measured family member, and decline others for stated
reasons.
\end{abstract}
