\begin{abstract}
Screening half-Heusler thermoelectrics needs the lattice thermal conductivity ($\kappa_L$) that a
laboratory will actually measure. First-principles calculations, and the machine-learning models
trained on them, reproduce calculated values instead, and how far these lie from experiment has not
been established across the class. Building a curated corpus that keeps calculations and
measurements apart, we find that published calculations systematically overestimate measured
$\kappa_L$, by a median factor of \num{1.69}, in almost every bonding family. We therefore predict
$\kappa_L$ in two steps: a machine-learning model estimates the calculated value from the crystal
structure, and a simple two-parameter transfer function maps it onto the experimental scale. Tested
on compounds whose chemistry was withheld from every fitted step, the method predicts measured
$\kappa_L$ with a median error of \SI{33.1}{\percent}, and \SI{87.2}{\percent} of compounds fall
within a factor of two of experiment, inside a stated domain of applicability (18 valence
electrons, cubic, no other polymorph). It outperforms baselines that ignore the identity of the
compound; over all 50 measured compounds, without the domain screens, the median error is
\SI{37.4}{\percent}. Where a bonding family has been measured, a constant fitted to that family
calibrates published calculations more closely (\SI{21.7}{\percent} against \SI{32.3}{\percent} held
out), although not significantly compound by compound, and passes a held-out test in five families. We issue ten predictions with uncertainty intervals
for unmeasured compounds, decline others for stated reasons, and predict two compounds with no
published report from their crystal structure alone.
\end{abstract}
