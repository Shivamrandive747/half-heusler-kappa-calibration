Measuring the offset fairly, and then correcting it, needs data in which calculations and
measurements are never mixed and tests in which the compound being predicted is withheld. The
prediction itself, of the \emph{measured} lattice thermal conductivity of a half Heusler, is made in
two steps. A machine-learning model predicts the first-principles (Boltzmann-transport-equation, BTE)
conductivity, $\kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}$, from the crystal structure (Section~\ref{sec:model}), and a
transfer function maps a first-principles value onto the experimental scale
(Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). Where a published calculation exists, the transfer function is applied to it directly and the model
is not used. We call the model followed by the transfer function the \emph{model route}, and the
transfer function applied to a published calculation the \emph{published route}. Measurements and calculations are kept apart from the
corpus onward and meet only at the transfer function (Figure~\ref{fig:method}). Rules given here in
outline are stated in full in the Extended Methods (Section~S1 of the Supplementary material).
Section, table and figure numbers that begin with S refer to the Supplementary material throughout.

\paragraph{Notation and conventions} A half Heusler is written XYZ: X is the most electropositive
element (a rare earth or an early transition metal), Y the late transition metal and Z the
main-group element. A bonding family is labelled by its Y and Z elements in no meaningful order, as
in \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}. A \emph{member} of a family is a compound of that family with both a usable
reference measurement and a temperature-matched published calculation. Formulas list the elements in order of increasing electronegativity, as the
descriptors do, so the compound often written TmPtSb in the literature appears here as \ce{TmSbPt}.
The \emph{adopted} constants are those used for the issued predictions. Table~\ref{tab:notation}
collects the symbols and labels, including the three prediction statuses
(Sections~\ref{sec:screening} and~\ref{sec:refusals}).

\subsection{Corpus construction}
\label{sec:dataset}

A correction from calculation to experiment can be fitted only on compounds that have both, so the
corpus is built to find those pairs and keeps calculations and measurements as separate records. It
holds \num{9495} temperature-resolved values for \num{960} distinct Heusler compounds, \num{735} of
them half Heuslers, and every value records its source and how it was obtained.

Measured values come from the Starrydata database of transport data digitised from the
literature~\cite{katsura2025starrydata} and from direct extraction from primary publications by
multimodal large language models (Google Gemini, \texttt{gemini-2.5-flash} and
\texttt{gemini-2.5-pro}; extraction counts in Section~S1 of the Supplementary material). No value enters the corpus on the model's authority alone: each must pass
the physical and provenance checks below.

Calculated values come from AFLOW~\cite{curtarolo2012aflow} (its Automatic Anharmonic Phonon Library,
AAPL~\cite{plata2017an}, and the Debye--Gr\"{u}neisen estimates of its Automatic Gibbs Library,
AGL~\cite{toher2014highthroug},
kept apart by the tiers of Section~\ref{sec:tiering}), the Materials
Project~\cite{jain2013commentary}, JARVIS~\cite{choudhary2020the}, OQMD~\cite{kirklin2015the} and
published phonon-transport studies. Crystal structures come from the same repositories, the
Crystallography Open Database~\cite{graulis2009crystallog} and OPTIMADE-federated
sources~\cite{andersen2021optimade}.

Experiments measure the total thermal conductivity, so most measured rows obtain the lattice part by
Wiedemann--Franz subtraction,
%
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_L = \kappa_\mathrm{tot} - \frac{L T}{\rho},
  \label{eq:wf}
\end{equation}
%
with $T$ the temperature, $\rho$ the electrical resistivity and the Lorenz number from the
single-parabolic-band expression of Kim \emph{et al.}~\cite{kim2015characteri},
$L = [1.5 + \exp(-|S|/116)]\times\SI{e-8}{\watt\ohm\per\kelvin\squared}$ with the Seebeck coefficient
$S$ in \si{\micro\volt\per\kelvin}. Rows without a Seebeck coefficient use the degenerate limit,
\SI{2.44e-8}{\watt\ohm\per\kelvin\squared}, which overstates the electronic part of a semiconductor
and so biases its lattice value low. The resistivity of a dense intermetallic lies between
\SI{e-8}{\ohm\meter} and \SI{e-2}{\ohm\meter}. A row outside that range cannot give a valid
subtraction by Eq.~\eqref{eq:wf}; in practice it is a total conductivity filed under a lattice
label, and it is removed.

No result may be counted twice. A value quoted from another paper, a second digitisation of the same
sample and a data deposit that republishes curves are therefore not counted as separate
measurements, and a preprint is merged with its version of record (rules in Section~S1 of the Supplementary material). Figure~\ref{fig:corpus} summarises the corpus. Where a compound has been measured in more than one
publication (Fig.~\ref{fig:corpus}(b)), one measurement has to stand for it.

\subsection{The measured values and the choice of reference}
\label{sec:measured}

The transfer function maps onto an experimental scale, so each compound needs a single reference
measurement, chosen without sight of any calculation. Every reference is a stoichiometric parent
compound (each coefficient within \num{0.02} of an integer). This excludes the doped and alloyed
specimens that dominate the half-Heusler literature, so the correction measures the offset between a
calculation and a measurement of the same compound. That offset combines error in the calculation
with the effect of the specimen; Section~\ref{sec:interpretation} discusses the two.

We never blend the curves of several laboratories, since a blend is a curve nobody measured. A fixed
hierarchy chooses one reference laboratory per compound from the specimen and the temperature
coverage alone: the laboratory closest to the median curve when four or more report the compound,
otherwise the densest specimen, then the widest temperature range. A laboratory that contradicts a
consensus, a data deposit, a nanostructured specimen and a source with fewer than four temperatures
rank last but are never deleted. Within the chosen publication, each \SI{100}{\kelvin} bin takes the
median of its rows.

Two rules adjust this choice. A \emph{family-paper rule} makes one publication that measured two or
more members of a family their common reference, so that a family constant (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}) describes one kind of
specimen. It is dropped in the blind test and for the compound held out in the family test (both described
in Section~\ref{sec:nulls}), so that
a score never depends on which family-mates have been measured. A curve-shape test removes from the
calibration any curve whose slope signals a mis-subtracted electronic term. In the blind test a
compound whose every curve fails keeps one, ranked last, so that the scored population does not
depend on the test.

Of the \num{50} measured compounds, \num{26} have a single publication, so the hierarchy decides the
reference only for the rest. The sources record little about each specimen, so the correction
describes published practice on average, not the microstructure of any one specimen. The spread
between laboratories, against which the scores are read, is given in Section~\ref{sec:blind}.

\subsection{Method tiers and quality control}
\label{sec:tiering}

The calculated values need the same care as the measured ones. The transfer function is fitted to
full first-principles calculations only, so every row carries a
\emph{method tier} that records how its value was obtained. Tier~0 is a laboratory measurement
(\num{50} half Heuslers from \num{152} publications). Tier~1 is a full solution of the phonon
Boltzmann transport equation from first-principles third-order force constants (\num{284} half
Heuslers). Tier~2 is an approximate or reduced transport route, including a transport calculation
whose force constants come from a machine-learned interatomic potential. Tier~3 is a semi-empirical
or quasi-harmonic estimate: Slack~\cite{slack1979the}, Debye--Callaway~\cite{callaway1959model} or
AGL. The tier is assigned from the method string, never from a repository's label.

Five quality-control conditions, given with their reasons in Section~S1, are applied when the corpus
is generated: a cubic $C1_b$ record (space group 216) from a source that is not a
prototype-substitution database; a polymorph flag; separation of half from full Heuslers by the site
ratio; a \SI{200}{\kelvin} temperature floor, below which a boundary-free calculation and a
boundary-limited measurement are not the same quantity; and, for tier~1, a positive match for a
transport solution in the method string with no disclaimer attached. Only tier~1 enters the
calibration and, for half Heuslers, the training of the model.

\subsection{Domain of applicability}
\label{sec:domain}

The tiers settle which values are used; the domain settles which compounds. A transfer function for
\emph{lattice} thermal conductivity is meaningful only where lattice conduction dominates. The domain is therefore half Heuslers with 18 valence electrons, a cubic
$C1_b$ structure, and no other polymorph on record. Transition-metal half Heuslers are
semiconducting at a valence-electron count (VEC) of 18~\cite{graf2011simple}; away from it a large
electronic term must be removed from every measurement. $\mathrm{VEC}=18$ is also where the corpus
has support. In the count, lanthanides and actinides are taken as group~3 (full rule in Section~S1 of the Supplementary material). Rare-earth members based on Pt or Pd at $\mathrm{VEC}=18$ are often
narrow-gap or semimetallic, so for them the electronic subtraction is less secure. The structural
conditions follow because the calculation being calibrated is for the cubic phase.

Two further screens apply to prediction candidates only. A candidate containing \ce{Ce}, \ce{Sm},
\ce{Eu} or \ce{Yb} is flagged, not issued. In these elements the 4f level lies close to the Fermi
energy, so the valence can vary from compound to compound~\cite{lawrence1981valence} and differ from
the trivalent value assumed when counting electrons; a compound counted as $\mathrm{VEC}=18$ may
then not be an 18-electron semiconductor. The validation compound \ce{YbNiSb} is still scored. A
candidate containing a radioactive element is excluded, since the corpus holds no training example
of one.

All \num{50} measured half Heuslers are scored in the blind test; \num{44} have $\mathrm{VEC}=18$
and \num{39} also pass the structural conditions. These \num{39} form the in-domain set, and the
same screens are applied to every prediction candidate. Table~\ref{tab:populations} lists the
populations that recur in the paper; results for different populations are not directly comparable.

\subsection{Training data, descriptors and the machine-learning model}
\label{sec:trainingdata}
\label{sec:model}

The first of the two steps is the model. Where no calculation is published it must supply one, so it
is trained on calculations and never on measurements. Its label is the phonon Boltzmann-transport conductivity of a cubic crystal,
%
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}} \;=\; \frac{1}{3N_{\mathbf{q}}V}\sum_{\mathbf{q},s}
    C_{\mathbf{q}s}\, v_{\mathbf{q}s}^{2}\, \tau_{\mathbf{q}s},
  \label{eq:bte}
\end{equation}
%
a sum over the $N_{\mathbf{q}}$ sampled wavevectors $\mathbf{q}$ and branches $s$ of the mode heat
capacity $C_{\mathbf{q}s}$, squared group velocity $v_{\mathbf{q}s}^{2}$ and lifetime
$\tau_{\mathbf{q}s}$, divided by the cell volume $V$. The training pool holds \num{681} labels on
\num{463} compounds; of the labels, \num{544} are at \SI{300}{\kelvin} and \num{119} at
\SI{500}{\kelvin}. Of the compounds, \num{284} are half Heuslers (\num{230} with
$\mathrm{VEC}=18$), each labelled by a tier-1 calculation. Full Heuslers share the descriptors and
also enter the pool; those without a transport calculation enter through a semi-empirical estimate
at reduced weight. Held-out scores are always computed on half Heuslers alone. The labels are
strongly right-skewed, so the model is fitted on $\log_{10}\kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}$ (distribution in
the Extended Methods).

The model is a set of gradient-boosted decision trees (CatBoost~\cite{prokhorenkova2017catboost}, chosen among four regressors in
Section~\ref{sec:modelselection}). Each prediction is the median over five random seeds. A few
diagnostics use seed~0 alone, the \emph{reference seed}, and are labelled as such; all main results
are medians over the five seeds.

\paragraph{Descriptors} Each row is described by \num{45} descriptors of the composition, the cubic
cell and the temperature (grouped in Section~S1 of the Supplementary material): the mass, radius and
electronegativity of X, Y and Z, and combinations of these across sites; the cell geometry, with a
full-Heusler flag; bond lengths, left missing where they cannot be computed; and temperature, as
$T$, $1000/T$ and $\log_{10}T$. The lattice parameter itself is never a descriptor, because sources
mix the primitive and conventional cells.

\subsection{Model selection}
\label{sec:modelselection}

To see how much the choice of regressor matters, we compared CatBoost, LightGBM, a Gaussian process
and kernel ridge regression on identical rows and descriptors. The two tree models also receive the
same row weights; the two kernel models are fitted unweighted. Each was scored out of fold
with five-fold cross-validation grouped by element system (the set of elements in a compound). CatBoost has the highest $R^2$ and the lowest root-mean-square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) (Table~\ref{tab:models}; the scores are
defined in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}).
LightGBM has the lowest median error per compound, but by only \num{0.1} percentage points, too
little to separate the two, and we retain CatBoost. The Gaussian process is close behind and kernel
ridge clearly worse, so the choice among the leading three matters little for the fit to
calculations. These scores measure fit to calculated labels over the whole pool, on element-system
folds and not the X-site clusters of the validation, so they are not comparable with the
half-Heusler accuracy of Section~\ref{sec:mlrepro}. Parity plots for all four are in Section~S4 of
the Supplementary material.

\subsection{The transfer function}
\label{sec:transfer}

The second step takes a calculation, published or supplied by the model, onto the experimental
scale. A laboratory specimen scatters phonons at grain boundaries, point defects and pores that a
calculation of the perfect crystal lacks; for the \ce{XNiSn} half Heuslers, grain-boundary and
point-defect scattering have been compared directly against measurement~\cite{schrade2017the}. Its
conductivity is therefore lower, and the
specimen contribution should grow as temperature falls and intrinsic mean free paths lengthen.

\paragraph{The shared form} We describe the offset empirically with two parameters:
%
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_L^{\mathrm{exp}}(T) \;=\; \kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}(T)\;
    \min\!\left[c\left(\frac{T}{300}\right)^{p},\,1\right].
  \label{eq:transfer}
\end{equation}
%
Here $c$ sets the room-temperature reduction and $p$ its temperature dependence. The cap at one is a
modelling choice for the shared form, which keeps a calibrated value from exceeding its calculation;
it is not a physical requirement, since part of the offset is error in the calculation, which can
have either sign. The constants are fitted on published tier-1 calculations, each paired with the
reference measurement of the same compound, with no model involved. Such pairs exist for
\num{34} in-domain compounds (\num{429} pairs). The fit weights every compound equally:
%
\begin{equation}
  (c, p) \;=\; \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c,\,p}\;
    \operatorname*{median}_{i \in \mathcal{C}}\;
    \operatorname*{median}_{j}
    \left|\log_{10}\frac{\hat{\kappa}(T_{ij};c,p)}{\kappa(T_{ij})}\right|,
  \label{eq:fit}
\end{equation}
%
where $\mathcal{C}$ is the set of compounds used in the fit, $\hat{\kappa}$ the corrected
calculation and $\kappa$ the reference measurement, compared at the temperatures $T_{ij}$ where both
exist. In words, the fit chooses the $c$ and $p$ that bring the corrected calculations closest to
measurement. For each compound we take the absolute logarithm of the ratio of corrected calculation
to measurement at each matched temperature, and the median over those temperatures; we then take the
median over compounds. The logarithm treats a value twice too high and one twice too low as equally
wrong. Taking the median within each compound first makes every compound count once, however many
temperatures it was measured at, and medians keep a single bad point, such as a value misread from a
digitised figure, from pulling the fit.

No compound is scored with a constant that has seen it. Before each compound is scored, the
constants are refitted without it and, where the test requires, without its relatives: without its
whole chemistry cluster in the blind test, without its whole bonding family in the
unseen-family test, and without the compound alone in the family test (the three
tests are described in Section~\ref{sec:nulls}). Fitted on all \num{34} compounds, the constants are $c=\num{0.42}$ and
$p=\num{0.70}$. At \SI{300}{\kelvin} the correction therefore multiplies the calculation by
\num{0.42}; it weakens as temperature rises and disappears at \SI{1036}{\kelvin}, where
$c\,(T/300)^{p}$ reaches one.

The size of the correction is known better than its temperature dependence. Repeating the fit on
\num{200} bootstrap resamples of the compounds, $c$ stays between \num{0.35} and \num{0.55}
(10th--90th percentile), whereas $p$ ranges from \num{0.45} to \num{1.15}. Refitting without one
chemistry cluster or one family at a time gives the same picture (Section~S1). The size of the correction is therefore better determined than its change with temperature;
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states the consequence: the method gives $\kappa_L$ at a stated
temperature and is not designed to reproduce the shape of $\kappa_L(T)$.

\paragraph{The family form} The offset may differ from one bonding family to another, so every bonding family with at least one
member that has both a published calculation and a usable reference measurement also carries its
own constant:
%
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_L^{\mathrm{exp}}(T) \;=\; \kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}(T)\;
    c_f\left(\frac{T}{300}\right)^{p}, \qquad p = \num{0.70}.
  \label{eq:family}
\end{equation}
%
Twelve families qualify. Only the magnitude is refitted, by Eq.~\eqref{eq:fit} restricted to
$c_f<1$ on the family's measured members, because most families are measured over too narrow a
range to identify their own exponent. A family with two or more measured members (nine families) is
tested held out: each member is predicted from the others with its own measurement withheld, and the
shared exponent is refitted without it. One guard acts on the fit and not on the score. In a family
with four or more members (\ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt} and \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb}), a member whose ratio of calculated
to measured $\kappa_L$ near \SI{300}{\kelvin} lies more than three robust standard deviations from the
family median, on a logarithmic scale and with the spread taken from the median absolute deviation, is
left out when $c_f$ is fitted; it is still predicted and scored. The guard is applied to the family as
a whole, held-out member included, and it acts once, on \ce{YSbPt} in \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}
(Section~\ref{sec:familycal}). The family is \emph{validated} only if the median error over
its folds is under \SI{25}{\percent}; otherwise it is \emph{calibrated, not validated}, its constant
describing each member's own reference without transferring between members, and no prediction is
issued through it. A family with one member is \emph{anchored}: its error is an in-sample fit, never
a validation. The family form has no cap. At high temperature it can exceed the calculation by up to
a factor of \num{2.18}, which is where the measurements of those families lie; every issued
prediction carries a factor below one.

A compound receives its family's constant wherever its family has a measured member, and the shared
constant otherwise. The leave-one-chemistry-out blind test is the exception: it is scored with the
shared constant, because a chemistry holdout leaves a compound's family-mates in training and would
not test a family constant at all.

\paragraph{Pairing a calculation with a measurement} Both forms are fitted on pairs of one calculation and one measurement, and the two can be compared
only at the same temperature. Measurements usually cover many temperatures, but most published
calculations are reported at only one. Where a calculation is reported at several temperatures, we
join its points by straight lines on a log--log plot and read off the value we need. Where it is
reported at a single temperature, we extend it by assuming $\kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}} \propto 1/T$, the
standard high-temperature behaviour when phonons are scattered mainly by one another (three-phonon
Umklapp scattering). We extend it by no more than a factor of \num{2.5} in temperature either way and
never below \SI{200}{\kelvin}, where this behaviour no longer holds; a value calculated at
\SI{300}{\kelvin} is thus used between \SI{200}{\kelvin} and \SI{750}{\kelvin}. Only one tier-1
calculation in the corpus spans three or more temperatures, so for nearly every pair the temperature
dependence of the calculation is this assumed $1/T$, not a computed one. When a compound has several
independent calculations, we use their median. If they disagree by more than a factor of two at some
temperature, that temperature is \emph{contested} and is not used, for measured compounds and
prediction candidates alike. A compound whose every calculated temperature is contested keeps the one
calculation closest to its family-mates' uncontested values (Section~S1). This is why \ce{TiNiPb} is quoted at \SI{500}{\kelvin}: its two
calculations at \SI{300}{\kelvin} differ by a factor of \num{5.6}.

\subsection{Predictions from crystal structure alone}
\label{sec:structureonly}

For a compound nobody has calculated, the model supplies $\kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}$ and the transfer
function maps it onto the experimental scale. We searched DxMag~\cite{xiao2025highthroug}, AFLOW,
OQMD, JARVIS and the Materials Project for cubic $C1_b$ cells inside the domain and took the lattice
parameter of the lowest-energy site ordering on record. A candidate is predicted only if its family,
its X element and every element are represented among the training half Heuslers. A prediction is
graded \emph{strong} when the model reproduces the family's own published calculations to within
\SI{25}{\percent} held out and the five seeds agree within a factor of \num{1.3}, \emph{moderate}
within \SI{50}{\percent} and \num{1.5}, and \emph{weak} otherwise. Literature status was checked in
OpenAlex~\cite{priem2022openalex}, Crossref and arXiv with a positive control; values reported only
in tables or supplements are outside the reach of that search, and the novelty statements here are
made on that basis. The two steps, structure to calculation and calculation to experiment, carry
errors that we report side by side and never combine. The full procedure and two model-free
cross-checks are in the Extended Methods.

\subsection{Validation, null baselines and prediction intervals}
\label{sec:nulls}
\label{sec:intervals}

Every step above is fitted to data, so each route must be tested on compounds it has not seen. Three
choices define the test: which compounds are hidden from the route, what it is compared against, and
how the uncertainty of each prediction is set.

\paragraph{Protocol} The final evaluation was specified in writing before it was run, and the dated
specification is archived with the code. It fixes how the shared constant is defined and held out,
the rule that each compound has a single reference measurement, how the intervals are built at the
quoted temperature, and which results are reported whatever they show. Earlier development runs on
the same compounds did shape some choices: the rules for building the corpus, the rule for choosing a
family's paper, the uncapped family form and the decision to apply the prediction screens to the
validation set. For that reason the main result is also reported without the screens
(Section~\ref{sec:blind}).

\paragraph{Held-out testing} A model can look accurate simply because it has already seen a close
relative of the compound it is asked to predict. To rule this out, every result for the model route
is obtained with the target compound's \emph{chemistry cluster} withheld. Half Heuslers are grouped by
their X element into chemistry clusters (all Ti half Heuslers together, for example), and a whole
cluster is removed at once, from the model and from the shared constant alike
(\emph{leave-one-chemistry-out}); this is the \emph{blind test}. Full Heuslers in the training set are
grouped by their doubled element and stay in training, so a full Heusler that contains the X element
is not removed. Holding out
whole clusters in this way tests whether a model can extrapolate to chemistry it has not
seen~\cite{durdy2022random}; conventional cross-validation overestimates how well a model does on
new materials~\cite{meredig2018can}. Family-mates with a different X element stay in training, so
the test measures accuracy for a new X element within a known family (numbers of clusters in Section~S1).
In the \emph{family test} the family constants are tested one compound at a time: each compound is
predicted from its family
constant fitted without it and from the shared constant refitted without it, and a sign
test~\cite{dixon1946the} counts how often each comes closer to measurement. In the \emph{unseen-family test} the shared constant is tested on families with no measured member
by removing one whole family at a time from the \num{34} compounds of the published route.

\paragraph{Null baselines} To show that the route has learned something about each compound, we
compare it with two \emph{null baselines} that know nothing about the compound: a single constant
and a simple temperature trend $A(T/300)^{q}$, both fitted to the training measurements. Each
baseline is fitted in two ways. In the first, fixed with the protocol, it is fitted to the individual
measurements: the constant is their median and the trend a least-squares line in logarithms. In the
second it is fitted as the constants of the route are, by minimising the median over compounds of
each compound's median error on a logarithmic scale (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). The second is the
fairer comparison, because under the first a compound measured at many temperatures weighs more in
the baseline than in the route; it was added after the protocol, and its specification was written
before it was run. A route that cannot beat these has learned nothing
useful. Both baselines use the same held-out chemistry and the same reference measurements as the
route. Each is compared with the route compound by compound with the Wilcoxon signed-rank
test~\cite{wilcoxon1945individual}, which asks whether the route is closer to measurement more often,
and by larger margins, than chance would allow. Because two comparisons are made, the $p$-values are
adjusted with Holm's correction, and the comparison is repeated with each chemistry cluster counted
once, so that a large cluster cannot dominate.

\paragraph{Prediction intervals} Each prediction comes with a \emph{prediction interval}: a range
of conductivity within which the measurement is expected to fall. Its width is set by how far the
route missed on compounds it had not seen. We use the cross-validation (jackknife) form of conformal
prediction~\cite{lei2018distributi}. In plain terms, we collect the held-out errors, sort them by
size, and take the error that a chosen fraction of compounds stay within. Errors are measured as
ratios on a logarithmic scale, so an interval extends by the same factor above and below the
prediction. The errors used are those of the model route, model step included, and the same intervals
are attached to
the issued predictions, which start from a published calculation. Each in-domain compound
contributes one error, in the \SI{100}{\kelvin} temperature bin closest to the quoted temperature,
under the correction it would actually receive: its family constant fitted without it, or else the
shared constant refitted without its chemistry cluster. For $m$ held-out compounds and a target
coverage $\alpha$ (for example $0.9$, meaning nine predictions in ten should fall inside), the
half-width of the interval is the $k$th smallest error,
%
\begin{equation}
  \delta_\alpha \;=\; \Bigl\{\, \bigl|\log_{10} R_i \bigr| \,\Bigr\}_{(k)},
  \qquad k = \bigl\lceil (m+1)\,\alpha \bigr\rceil,
  \label{eq:conformal}
\end{equation}
%
where $R_i$ is the ratio of prediction to reference for compound $i$ in that bin. The interval runs
from the prediction divided by $10^{\delta_\alpha}$ to the prediction multiplied by it. The $+1$ in
$k$ is a small-sample correction that makes the interval slightly wider than the plain percentile.

\paragraph{Coverage} Every issued prediction carries such an interval (Table~\ref{tab:predictions}),
and a reader will expect a \SI{90}{\percent} interval to contain the measured value about nine times
in ten. \emph{Coverage}, the fraction of compounds whose measurement actually falls inside their
interval, tests whether it does. It must be measured here, not assumed: split conformal prediction
guarantees its stated coverage under ideal conditions~\cite{lei2018distributi}, but the jackknife
form carries no such guarantee~\cite{barber2021predictive}, and compounds that share a chemistry
cluster or a laboratory are not fully independent. Each compound is therefore tested against an
interval built only from the errors of the other chemistry clusters, and the median over seeds is
used. At \SI{300}{\kelvin} the intervals agree with their nominal levels: the \SI{50}{\percent} interval spans a factor of
\num{1.62} either way and the \SI{90}{\percent} interval a factor of \num{2.85}, and they contain
\num{20} (\SI{51}{\percent}) and \num{36} (\SI{92}{\percent}) of the \num{39} in-domain compounds.
At \SI{500}{\kelvin} they span factors of \num{1.38} and \num{1.89} (all levels in Section~S1).
The test is a weak one: each interval is built from the errors of the same \num{39} compounds with
one cluster removed, so agreement close to the nominal level is expected, and the test would expose
only a cluster whose errors differ markedly from the rest.
Families with one measured member or none are too thin for this test, so their intervals are
untested.

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}
\label{sec:definitions}

The tests above are scored in two ways. When choosing the machine-learning model, we score how well it
reproduces the calculated values row by row, on the logarithm $y=\log_{10}\kappa_L$, with the usual
RMSE, MAE and $R^2$ (defined in Section~S1). An RMSE of \num{0.270} on the logarithm means the model
typically misses a calculated value by a factor of about $10^{0.270}\approx\num{1.86}$. Every other
number in the paper, including every comparison with measurement, is computed \emph{per compound},
as follows.

The error of a prediction $\hat{\kappa}$ against the reference measurement $\kappa$ at temperature
$T$ is the absolute percentage error,
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{APE}(T) \;=\; \frac{\bigl|\hat{\kappa}(T) - \kappa(T)\bigr|}{\kappa(T)},
  \label{eq:ape}
\end{equation}
%
so a prediction of \SI{6}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} against a measurement of
\SI{4}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} has an error of \SI{50}{\percent}. A compound measured at several
temperatures $T_{ij}$ receives a single error $e_i$ and a single ratio $r_i$, the medians over those
temperatures:
%
\begin{equation}
  e_i \;=\; \operatorname*{median}_{j=1\ldots n_i} \mathrm{APE}(T_{ij}),
  \qquad
  r_i \;=\; \operatorname*{median}_{j} \frac{\hat{\kappa}(T_{ij})}{\kappa(T_{ij})}.
  \label{eq:err}
\end{equation}
%
From these we report four things. The \emph{median error} is the middle value of $e_i$ over
compounds. The fraction \emph{within a factor of two} counts compounds whose prediction lies between
half and twice the measurement ($\tfrac{1}{2} \le r_i \le 2$). The fractions \emph{within
\SI{30}{\percent}} ($e_i \le 0.30$) and \emph{within \SI{25}{\percent}} ($e_i < 0.25$, the bar a
family must pass to count as validated) count compounds with small errors. The \emph{bias},
$\operatorname{median}_i r_i$, shows whether predictions are too high (above one) or too low (below
one) overall.

Counting each compound once stops a compound measured at many temperatures from outweighing one
measured at few; the correction is fitted the same way for the same reason. The percentage error is
lopsided: predicting twice the measurement counts as \SI{100}{\percent}, predicting half of it as
only \SI{50}{\percent}. The within-a-factor-of-two fraction treats both the same, so it is always
reported beside the median error.

The model is trained five times with different random seeds. The \emph{five-seed median} is the
median of the five per-seed median errors. Where a paired test is quoted ``at the median over
seeds'', the number is the median of the five per-seed $p$-values; it summarises the seeds but is not
itself a $p$-value, so the range over seeds is given where it matters.
