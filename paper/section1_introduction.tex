\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A thermoelectric generator needs a material that conducts charge freely but obstructs heat. Half
Heuslers meet the first requirement far better than the second. Their electronic performance is
well established, with figures of merit approaching $zT \approx 1.5$ in $p$-type
\ce{NbFeSb}-based~\cite{fu2015realizing} and $zT \approx 1.4$ in \ce{ZrCoBi}-based
alloys~\cite{zhu2018discovery}. Their lattice thermal conductivity $\kappa_L$, however, remains
high, and because $zT$ varies inversely with $\kappa_L$, most advances have come from suppressing
it after synthesis. The screening problem is to identify compounds with intrinsically low
$\kappa_L$ before they are made.

That requires a calculated $\kappa_L$. Solving the phonon Boltzmann
transport equation (BTE) from first principles gives $\kappa_L$ with no experimental input; for
the half Heusler \ce{ZrCoSb} the calculated temperature dependence agrees reasonably with
measurement, running slightly above it, with no fitted parameter~\cite{shiomi2011thermal}. The
obstacle is cost. Carrete \emph{et al.} calculated \num{32} half Heuslers in full and trained a
random-forest model on them to search a far larger set~\cite{carrete2014finding}, and much
subsequent work has approximated the calculation at lower cost, through compressed-sensing
descriptors~\cite{liu2020a} and machine-learning models trained on computed
conductivities~\cite{luo2023predicting,trans2022lattice,miyazaki2021machine,jaafreh2021lattice,bhattacharjee2022thorough}.
Most recently, Paliwal and Alam compared seven tree-based ensemble regressors trained on \num{4127}
calculated values for \num{150} compounds; the best, an extra-trees model, reached
$R^2 = \num{0.9994}$ (on $\log\kappa_L$) in repeated cross-validation on calculated
labels~\cite{paliwal2025accelerate}.

We call such models \emph{surrogates}, because they stand in for the calculation. A surrogate is
accurate against the quantity it was trained on, and that quantity is a calculation, not a
measurement. How far the two differ has been judged in two ways. An earlier tutorial on the method
stated that it ``has enabled thermal conductivity predictions that agree with experimental
measurements for diverse crystalline materials''~\cite{mcgaughey2019phonon}. A later controlled
multi-code comparison, by the same lead author, found the solvers within \SI{15}{\percent} of
their mean values, yet reported that the calculated conductivities ``do not generally show
agreement with experimental measurements, which is attributed to the limitations of the density
functional theory calculations''~\cite{mcgaughey2025phonon}. Paliwal and Alam note that
computed $\kappa_L$ ``often overestimates the experimental values because real samples inevitably
contain impurities, defects, and grain boundaries''~\cite{paliwal2025accelerate}, but they do not
quantify this \emph{offset} between calculation and measurement. What has been missing, to our
knowledge, is the size and sign of the offset across a materials class, and a correction for it
validated against measurements withheld from its fit.

In our corpus, published transport calculations exceed the measured $\kappa_L$ of the same compound
by a median factor of \num{1.69} over \num{34} compounds. A recent large-scale model shows what
that means for a surrogate. The Elemental Spatial Density Neural Network Force Field
(Elemental-SDNNFF)~\cite{rodriguez2023millionsca} replaces first-principles force calculations with
a neural network and so predicts the phonon properties of \num{11866} Heusler structures, a scale
out of reach of direct calculation. For the measured half Heuslers of our validation set that also
have a published first-principles calculation, its values agree closely with that calculation
(median ratio \num{0.99} over \num{18} compounds). Against the laboratory measurements of the
\num{21} it shares with the same set, however, they are a median \num{2.01} times too high
(Section~\ref{sec:sdnnff}). The force field does well what it was built to do; it is the target
itself that lies away from experiment.

The data-driven routes from calculation to measurement that we know of do not correct a full
first-principles calculation.
Zhu \emph{et al.} transfer-learned a crystal-graph network from \num{2668} computed conductivities
onto \num{132} measured values, starting from semi-empirical high-throughput
estimates~\cite{zhu2021charting}. Miller \emph{et al.} fitted a semi-empirical model to
experiment~\cite{miller2017capturing}, Chen \emph{et al.} trained a Gaussian process on about one
hundred measured solids~\cite{chen2019machine}, and a related study pretrained on empirical-model
estimates and then fine-tuned on a single high-fidelity target that pools measurements with
first-principles calculations~\cite{liu2022leveraging}. Pooling hides the offset. For band gaps,
learning the difference between calculation and measurement has been shown to work better than
pooling mixed sources~\cite{hutchinson2017overcoming}; we take the same view for thermal
conductivity and keep experiment and calculation apart so that the offset can be measured. Our
correction acts on the first-principles calculation itself.

Measurements to correct against are scarce. Of the \num{735} half Heuslers in our corpus with any
thermal-conductivity record, \num{284} have a full first-principles transport calculation and
\num{50} have been measured. Models trained on large public thermal-conductivity datasets are
beginning to appear~\cite{barua2024interpreta}, but \num{50} compounds are too few to learn a
structure--property relation from scratch; they are, as we show, enough to calibrate a correction
to calculated values.

We therefore predict the measured $\kappa_L$ in two steps. A machine-learning model, itself a
surrogate trained on calculations, predicts the full first-principles value from crystal
structure, and a transfer function maps a calculation onto the experimental scale; where a
calculation has been published, the transfer function is applied to it directly. The
\emph{domain of applicability} of the method is cubic half Heuslers with 18 valence electrons
(Section~\ref{sec:domain}). Each measured compound is scored against one reference laboratory
chosen by a fixed rule that never reads a calculation. With a half Heusler written as XYZ
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), a \emph{bonding family} is the set of compounds that share Y and Z,
and a \emph{chemistry cluster} the set that share X. The \emph{blind test} runs the two steps end
to end with each compound's chemistry cluster held out of both; Table~\ref{tab:notation} defines
the terms used throughout.

The paper makes four contributions, taken in the order of the Results
(Section~\ref{sec:results}).
%
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt, leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \emph{The offset, measured.} Published transport calculations run high in \num{29} of
        \num{34} half Heuslers, and the offset shrinks as temperature rises
        (Section~\ref{sec:offset}).
  \item \emph{A closed-form correction.} A transfer function with a magnitude and a temperature
        exponent (Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer}), fitted on published calculations, has magnitude
        $c = \num{0.42}$, better constrained than the exponent $p = \num{0.70}$. It carries over
        to a separately computed set of calculations, and a magnitude refitted per bonding family
        is validated held out in five of the nine families with two or more measured members
        (Section~\ref{sec:transferresults}).
  \item \emph{An end-to-end blind test against experiment.} With a model that reproduces published
        calculations to \SI{21.9}{\percent} (reference seed; Section~\ref{sec:mlrepro}), the two
        steps together predict measured $\kappa_L$ in the blind test to a median error of
        \SI{33.1}{\percent} inside the domain of applicability (\SI{37.4}{\percent} over all
        \num{50} measured compounds). Their typical disagreement with measurement is similar in
        size to the spread between two laboratories measuring the same compound. They are closer
        to measurement than null baselines fitted to the individual measurements, which use no
        information about the compound, but not significantly closer than a constant fitted
        compound by compound, as their own constants are (Section~\ref{sec:blind}).
  \item \emph{Predictions that can be tested.} Ten issued predictions with intervals, \num{15}
        flagged candidates with their reasons, \num{10} estimates conditional on a measurement in
        their family, and values from crystal structure alone for \ce{TmPbAu} and \ce{HfTeOs}, which have neither a measurement nor a published full calculation in our corpus (Sections~\ref{sec:issued}
        to~\ref{sec:structonly}).
\end{enumerate}
