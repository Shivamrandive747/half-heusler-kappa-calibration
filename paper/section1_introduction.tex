\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A thermoelectric generator needs a material that conducts charge freely but obstructs heat. Half
Heuslers meet the first requirement far better than the second. Their electronic performance is
well established, with figures of merit approaching $zT \approx 1.5$ in $p$-type
\ce{NbFeSb}-based~\cite{fu2015realizing} and $zT \approx 1.4$ in \ce{ZrCoBi}-based
alloys~\cite{zhu2018discovery}, and
the class is mechanically robust, stable to high temperature and in many cases made of abundant
elements. Their
lattice thermal conductivity $\kappa_L$, however, remains high, and because $zT$ varies inversely
with $\kappa_L$, most advances have come from suppressing it after synthesis. The screening problem
is to identify compounds with intrinsically low $\kappa_L$ before they are made.

Solving the phonon Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) from first principles gives $\kappa_L$ with no
experimental input; for the half Heusler \ce{ZrCoSb} the calculated temperature dependence agrees
reasonably with measurement, running slightly above it, with no fitted parameter~\cite{shiomi2011thermal}.
The obstacle is cost. Carrete \emph{et al.} calculated
\num{32} half Heuslers in full and trained a random-forest model on them to search a far larger
set~\cite{carrete2014finding}, and much subsequent work has approximated the calculation at lower
cost,
through compressed-sensing descriptors~\cite{liu2020a} and machine-learned models trained on
computed
conductivities~\cite{luo2023predicting,trans2022lattice,miyazaki2021machine,jaafreh2021lattice,bhattacharjee2022thorough}.
Most recently, Paliwal and Alam compared seven tree-based ensemble regressors trained on \num{4127}
calculated values for \num{150} compounds; the best, an extra-trees model, reached
$R^2 = \num{0.9994}$ (on $\log\kappa_L$) in repeated cross-validation on calculated
labels~\cite{paliwal2025accelerate}.

We call such models \emph{surrogates}, because they stand in for the calculation. They are accurate
against the quantity they were trained on, which is a calculation rather than a measurement. How
far calculation and measurement differ has been judged in two ways. An earlier tutorial on the
method stated that it ``has enabled thermal conductivity predictions that agree with experimental
measurements for diverse crystalline materials''~\cite{mcgaughey2019phonon}; a later controlled multi-code comparison, by the same
lead author, found the solvers within \SI{15}{\percent} of their mean values, yet reported that the
calculated conductivities ``do not generally show agreement with experimental
measurements''~\cite{mcgaughey2025phonon}. Paliwal and Alam note that computed $\kappa_L$ ``often
overestimates the experimental values because real samples inevitably contain impurities, defects,
and grain boundaries''~\cite{paliwal2025accelerate}, but do not quantify the offset. What has been missing is the magnitude and sign of the disagreement across a
materials class, and a correction for it validated against held-out experiment.

In our corpus, published transport calculations exceed the measured $\kappa_L$ of the same compound
by a median factor of \num{1.69} over \num{34} compounds. A surrogate that reproduces the calculation
therefore carries that offset when its output is read as a prediction of experiment. A published
neural-network force field, Elemental-SDNNFF~\cite{rodriguez2023millionsca}, illustrates the
problem (Section~\ref{sec:sdnnff}). On the \num{21} compounds it shares with our validation set
inside the domain of applicability (Section~\ref{sec:domain}), its values are a median \num{2.01} times the measurements, while on the \num{18} that carry a
published calculation they are a median \num{0.99} times that calculation.

Existing routes across this gap do not correct a full BTE calculation. Zhu \emph{et al.}
transfer-learned a crystal-graph network from \num{2668} computed conductivities onto \num{132}
measured values, starting from semi-empirical high-throughput estimates~\cite{zhu2021charting}.
Miller \emph{et al.} fitted a semi-empirical model to experiment~\cite{miller2017capturing}, Chen
\emph{et al.} trained a Gaussian process on about one hundred measured solids~\cite{chen2019machine},
and a related study pretrained on empirical-model estimates and then fine-tuned on a single
high-fidelity target that pools measurements with first-principles
calculations~\cite{liu2022leveraging}. Pooling hides the offset. For band gaps, learning the
difference between calculation and measurement has been shown to work better than pooling mixed
sources~\cite{hutchinson2017overcoming}; we take the same view for thermal conductivity and keep
experiment and calculation apart so that the offset can be measured. Our correction acts on the
transport calculation itself and is closed-form: a magnitude and an exponent that can be applied by
hand.

Measurements to correct against are scarce. Of the \num{735} half Heuslers in our corpus with any
thermal-conductivity record, \num{284} have a full first-principles transport calculation and
\num{50} have been measured. Models trained on large public thermal-conductivity datasets are
beginning to appear~\cite{barua2024interpreta}, but \num{50} compounds are too few to learn a
structure--property relation from scratch. In our tests they are enough to calibrate a correction
to calculated values.

We therefore predict the measured $\kappa_L$ in two steps used as a pair: a machine-learned model,
trained on calculations, predicts the full first-principles value from crystal structure, and a
transfer function maps a calculation onto the experimental scale. Measurements and calculations are
never pooled, and each measured compound is scored against one reference laboratory chosen by a
fixed rule that never reads a calculation. With a half Heusler written as XYZ
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), a \emph{bonding family} is the set of compounds that share Y and Z, and
a \emph{chemistry cluster} the set that share X. The end-to-end test holds each compound's chemistry
cluster out of both steps; Table~\ref{tab:notation} defines the terms used throughout.

The paper makes four contributions.
%
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt, leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item \emph{The offset, measured.} Published transport calculations run high in \num{29} of
        \num{34} half Heuslers, by a median factor of \num{1.69}, and the excess shrinks as
        temperature rises (Section~\ref{sec:offset}).
  \item \emph{A closed-form correction.} A transfer function with a magnitude and a temperature
        exponent (Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer}), fitted on published calculations, has a well-determined
        magnitude $c = \num{0.42}$ and a poorly determined exponent $p = \num{0.70}$. It carries over to an independent set of calculations, and a
        magnitude refitted per bonding family is validated held out in five families
        (Section~\ref{sec:transferresults}).
  \item \emph{An end-to-end test against experiment.} Paired with a model that reproduces
        published calculations to \SI{21.9}{\percent} (reference seed), the route predicts measured
        $\kappa_L$ to a median error of \SI{33.1}{\percent} inside the domain of applicability, with
        each chemistry cluster held out of both steps. Its typical disagreement with measurement is
        similar in size to the spread between two laboratories measuring the same compound, and it
        beats compound-blind baselines (Section~\ref{sec:blind}).
  \item \emph{Predictions that can be tested.} Ten issued predictions with intervals, \num{15}
        flagged candidates with their reasons, \num{10} estimates conditional on a measurement in
        their family, and values from crystal structure alone for \ce{TmPbAu} and \ce{HfTeOs}, for
        which our literature search finds no published report (Sections~\ref{sec:issued}
        to~\ref{sec:structonly}).
\end{enumerate}
