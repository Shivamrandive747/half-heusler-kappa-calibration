\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A thermoelectric generator needs a material that conducts charge freely but obstructs heat. Half
Heuslers meet the first requirement far better than the second. Their electronic performance is
well established, with figures of merit approaching $zT \approx 1.5$ in $p$-type
\ce{NbFeSb}-based~\cite{fu2015realizing} and $zT \approx 1.4$ in \ce{ZrCoBi}-based
alloys~\cite{zhu2018discovery}, and
the class is mechanically robust, stable to high temperature and in many cases made of abundant
elements. Their
lattice thermal conductivity $\kappa_L$, however, remains high, and because $zT$ varies inversely
with $\kappa_L$, most advances have come from suppressing it after synthesis. The screening problem
is to identify compounds with intrinsically low $\kappa_L$ before they are made.

Solving the phonon Boltzmann transport equation (BTE) from first principles gives $\kappa_L$ with no
experimental input, and reproduces the measured temperature dependence of a half Heusler without
being fitted to it~\cite{shiomi2011thermal}. The obstacle is cost. Carrete \emph{et al.} calculated
\num{32} half Heuslers in full and trained a random-forest model on them to search a far larger
set~\cite{carrete2014finding}, and much subsequent work has approximated the calculation at lower
cost,
through compressed-sensing descriptors~\cite{liu2020a} and machine-learned models trained on
databases of computed
conductivities~\cite{luo2023predicting,trans2022lattice,miyazaki2021machine,jaafreh2021lattice,bhattacharjee2022thorough}.
Most recently, Paliwal and Alam compared seven tree-based ensemble regressors trained on \num{4127}
calculated values for \num{150} compounds; the best, an extra-trees model, reached
$R^2 = \num{0.9994}$ (on $\log\kappa_L$) on held-out calculated
labels~\cite{paliwal2025accelerate}.

We call such models \emph{surrogates}, because they stand in for the calculation. They are accurate
against the quantity they were trained on, which is a calculation rather than a measurement. How
far calculation and measurement differ has been judged in two ways. An earlier review concluded that
first-principles transport ``agree[s] with experimental measurements for diverse crystalline
materials''~\cite{mcgaughey2019phonon}; a later controlled multi-code comparison, by the same
lead author, found the solvers within \SI{15}{\percent} of their mean values, yet reported that the
calculated conductivities ``do not generally show agreement with experimental
measurements''~\cite{mcgaughey2025phonon}. Paliwal and Alam note that computed $\kappa_L$ ``often
overestimates the experimental values because real samples inevitably contain impurities, defects,
and grain boundaries''~\cite{paliwal2025accelerate}, but leave the size of the effect
unmeasured. What has been missing is the magnitude and sign of the disagreement across a
materials class, and a correction for it validated against held-out experiment.

In our corpus, published transport calculations exceed the measured $\kappa_L$ of the same compound
by a median factor of \num{1.69} over \num{34} compounds. A surrogate that reproduces the calculation
therefore carries that offset when its output is read as a prediction of experiment. A published
neural-network force field, Elemental-SDNNFF~\cite{rodriguez2023millionsca}, illustrates the
problem (Section~\ref{sec:sdnnff}). On the \num{21} compounds it shares with our validation set
inside the domain of applicability (Section~\ref{sec:domain}), its values are a median \num{2.01} times the measurements, while on the \num{18} that carry a
published calculation they are a median \num{0.99} times that calculation.

Existing routes across this gap do not correct a full BTE calculation. Zhu \emph{et al.}
transfer-learned a crystal-graph network from \num{2668} computed conductivities onto \num{132}
measured values, starting from semi-empirical high-throughput estimates~\cite{zhu2021charting}.
Miller \emph{et al.} fitted a semi-empirical model to experiment~\cite{miller2017capturing}, Chen
\emph{et al.} trained a Gaussian process on about one hundred measured solids~\cite{chen2019machine},
and a related study pretrained on empirical-model estimates and then fine-tuned on a single
high-fidelity target that pools measurements with first-principles
calculations~\cite{liu2022leveraging}. Pooling the two hides the offset; we keep experiment and
calculation apart so that it can be measured. Our correction acts on the transport calculation itself and is closed-form: a magnitude
and an exponent that can be applied by hand.

Measurements to correct against are scarce. Of the \num{735} half Heuslers in our corpus with any
thermal-conductivity record, \num{284} have a full first-principles transport calculation and
\num{50} have been measured. Models trained on large literature-compiled thermal-conductivity
datasets are beginning to appear~\cite{barua2024interpreta}, but \num{50} compounds are too few to learn a structure--property
relation from scratch. In our tests they are enough to calibrate a correction to calculated values.

We predict the measured $\kappa_L$ with two steps used as a pair: a machine-learned model, trained on
calculations, predicts the full first-principles value from crystal structure, and a transfer
function maps a calculation onto the experimental scale. Measurements and calculations are never
pooled, and each measured compound is scored against one reference laboratory chosen by a fixed rule
that never reads a calculation. With a half Heusler written as XYZ (Section~\ref{sec:method}), a
\emph{bonding family} is the set of compounds that share Y and Z, and a \emph{chemistry cluster} the
set that share X. The end-to-end test holds each compound's chemistry cluster out of both steps.

A few terms recur throughout. The \emph{median error} is the typical percentage difference between
a prediction and the measured value: half of the compounds are predicted more closely than this. A
prediction lies \emph{within a factor of two} when it falls between half and twice the measured
value. A compound is \emph{held out} when nothing fitted to it has seen its own data, or, for a
chemistry cluster, the data of any compound in that cluster. The model is trained five times with
different random \emph{seeds}, and model-based results are the median over these five independently
trained models unless one seed is named. \emph{Null baselines} predict without any
compound-specific information, for instance a single constant conductivity; a useful method must
beat them. A \emph{conformal interval} is a range around a prediction whose width is
set by the errors observed on held-out compounds. Finally, every value carries a method
\emph{tier}: tier~0 for a measurement, tier~1 for a full first-principles transport calculation,
and tiers~2 and 3 for approximate and semi-empirical estimates (Section~\ref{sec:tiering}).

Our contributions are:
%
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*), itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt, leftmargin=2.2em]
  \item A machine-learned model, trained on calculations and never on measurements (every
        half-Heusler label a full first-principles calculation), that predicts
        $\kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}$ from crystal structure with \num{45} descriptors, selected among
        four regressors trained on identical rows and folds. With each chemistry cluster held out,
        it reproduces published calculations to a median error of \SI{21.9}{\percent} (reference
        seed),
        with \SI{88.3}{\percent} of \num{230} compounds within a factor of two, and its
        \textsc{shap} (SHapley Additive exPlanations) attribution is consistent with phonon physics.
  \item A closed-form transfer function, $\kappa_L^{\mathrm{exp}} = \kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}}
        \min[c\,(T/300)^p, 1]$, that corrects an offset consistent across the class (calculations
        run high in \num{29} of \num{34} compounds). Its shared magnitude $c = \num{0.42}$ is well
        determined and its exponent $p = \num{0.70}$ much less so. Applied to published
        calculations, the shared constant gives a median error of \SI{33.0}{\percent} on families
        never measured. A constant refitted per bonding family lowers the error on held-out family
        members from \SI{32.3}{\percent} to \SI{21.7}{\percent}, though not significantly compound by
        compound (sign test $p = \num{0.44}$), and is validated held out in five families.
  \item An end-to-end blind test from crystal structure to measurement. Inside the domain of
        applicability (18 valence electrons, a cubic $C1_b$ structure, no other polymorph on
        record), the median error is \SI{33.1}{\percent} and \SI{87.2}{\percent} of \num{39}
        compounds lie within a factor of two (median of five model seeds), against
        \SI{41.9}{\percent} uncorrected and \SIrange{45.6}{46.7}{\percent} for two null baselines
        (paired Wilcoxon $p = \num{0.004}$; marginal when each chemistry cluster is counted once).
        Without the domain screens, over all \num{50} measured compounds, the median error is
        \SI{37.4}{\percent}.
  \item Ten issued predictions with prediction intervals, three of them at \SI{500}{\kelvin}, the
        only temperature with a calculation that no independent calculation contradicts; \num{15} flagged
        candidates, each with its reason; and \num{10} estimates conditional on a measurement in
        their family. From crystal structure alone we also give values for \ce{TmPbAu} and
        \ce{HfTeOs}, for which our literature search finds no published report of any kind.
\end{enumerate}
