\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Used as a pair, machine learning and transfer-function calibration predict the measured lattice
thermal conductivity of half Heuslers from crystal structure. The machine-learned model reproduces
published first-principles calculations to a median error of \SI{21.9}{\percent} (reference seed) with each
chemistry cluster held out, and what it has learned is consistent with phonon physics. The transfer
function is needed because the calculations run high, by a median factor of \num{1.69} and in
\num{10} of \num{12} bonding families, and it can be closed-form because that offset is regular.

With each compound's chemistry cluster held out of both steps, and inside a domain of applicability
defined by the screens every candidate must pass, the median error is \SI{33.1}{\percent} and
\SI{87.2}{\percent} of \num{39} compounds lie within a factor of two, against \SI{41.9}{\percent}
uncorrected and \SI{46.7}{\percent} and \SI{45.6}{\percent} for constant and power-law null baselines.
The improvement is significant per compound on every model seed before correction for the two
comparisons; after a Holm correction $p = \num{0.007}$ at the median seed and \num{0.056} on the
least favourable one. Counted once per cluster, it passes
the Wilcoxon test on every seed ($p < 0.05$, largest \num{0.048}), but the sign test is not
significant on every seed, so the cluster-level evidence is marginal. The result does not depend on
\ce{YNiBi}, whose measured curve fails the curve-shape test, and without the domain screens, over all
\num{50} measured compounds, the same test gives \SI{37.4}{\percent} and still improves on both nulls
(Section~\ref{sec:blind}). The route performs
comparably to calibrating a published calculation (\SI{32.3}{\percent} on the \num{31} compounds of
the family test, a different set under a weaker holdout). Where a family has been measured, a power
law fitted to its measured members is as accurate (\SI{25.4}{\percent} against \SI{31.6}{\percent},
not significantly different), so the method's value lies in ordering compounds within a family and
in families never measured. The correction also carries over to an independent machine-learned
force field, lowering its median error against measurement from \SI{101.4}{\percent} to
\SI{36.8}{\percent}.

A magnitude refitted per bonding family is validated held out in five families, at
\SIrange{7.4}{24.9}{\percent}; in two of them (\ce{Co}--\ce{Sb} and \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb}) the shared
constant does as well or better on the same compounds, and the family constant is applied there by
the pre-specified rule. Four further families are calibrated on their own papers but do not transfer
between members held out, because their members are different materials as made; no predictions
are issued through them. The best result, \SI{7.4}{\percent} for \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}, rests on three
members from a single paper, each measured at only three temperatures. Pooled, the family
constant lowers the median error from \SI{32.3}{\percent} to \SI{21.7}{\percent}, but it is better for
\num{16} compounds, worse for \num{11} and tied for \num{4} (sign test $p = \num{0.44}$), so it is not
significantly better compound by compound.

We issue \num{10} predictions with intervals, flag \num{15} candidates with the reason named for each,
and report \num{10} estimates as conditional on a measurement in their family. From crystal structure
alone we give $\kappa_L$ at \SI{300}{\kelvin} of \SI{1.39}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} for
\ce{TmPbAu} and \SI{5.46}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} for \ce{HfTeOs}, compounds for which we find no
published report. Each carries two separate error estimates: \SI{21.9}{\percent} for the model and
\SI{33.0}{\percent} for the shared constant on families never measured.

The method estimates $\kappa_L$ at a stated temperature but does not predict its temperature
dependence: over a bootstrap of compounds the magnitude stays between \num{0.35} and \num{0.55} while
the exponent ranges from \num{0.45} to \num{1.15}, and at high temperature the measured lattice
values may retain part of the electronic contribution. Scores depend on the single reference laboratory
chosen per compound, in a field whose laboratories disagree by a median factor of \num{1.33}, and the
compounds share clusters, families and laboratories. A family constant interpolates within its
family, three families (\ce{Co}--\ce{Bi}, \ce{Ni}--\ce{Pb} and \ce{Sb}--\ce{Ir}) rest on a single
measured compound, and the intervals are validated only on
in-domain compounds. Seven of the ten issued predictions rest on a single calculation source at the
quoted temperature. The method should not be used outside its domain.

A single synthesis and measurement of the cubic phase can falsify any issued prediction. Four kinds
of measurement would most improve the method: a first measurement in a family with no measured
member, such as \ce{Pb}--\ce{Au} or \ce{Te}--\ce{Os}; a second, bulk member from another laboratory in
each family that rests on one compound or one paper; wide-range measurements on dense specimens to
constrain the exponent; and two sound \ce{Ni}--\ce{Bi} measurements. Free of the bipolar
contribution that rules out the existing \ce{YNiBi} curve, these would allow nine of the ten
conditional estimates to be issued. For this class the binding constraint is experimental: what the conditional estimates lack
is a measured member of their family, not a calculation.
