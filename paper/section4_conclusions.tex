\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Used as a pair, machine learning and transfer-function calibration predict the measured lattice
thermal conductivity of half Heuslers from crystal structure. The machine-learned model reproduces
published first-principles calculations to a median error of \SI{21.9}{\percent} (reference seed) with each
chemistry cluster held out, and its attributions are consistent with phonon physics. The transfer
function is needed because the calculations run high, by a median factor of \num{1.69} and in
\num{10} of \num{12} bonding families, and it can be closed-form because that offset is regular.

With each compound's chemistry cluster held out of both steps, and inside a domain of applicability
defined by the screens every candidate must pass, the median error is \SI{33.1}{\percent} and
\SI{87.2}{\percent} of \num{39} compounds lie within a factor of two, against \SI{41.9}{\percent}
uncorrected and \SI{46.7}{\percent} and \SI{45.6}{\percent} for constant and power-law null baselines.
The improvement is significant compound by compound (Holm-corrected $p = \num{0.007}$ at the median
seed, \num{0.056} on the least favourable) and marginal when each chemistry cluster is counted once.
Without the domain screens, over all \num{50} measured compounds, the same test gives
\SI{37.4}{\percent} and still improves on both nulls. Where a family has already been measured, a
power law fitted to its members is as accurate (\SI{25.4}{\percent} against \SI{31.6}{\percent}, not
significantly different), so the method adds most in ordering compounds within a family and in
families never measured. The correction also carries over to an independent machine-learned force
field, lowering its median error against measurement from \SI{101.4}{\percent} to
\SI{36.8}{\percent}.

A magnitude refitted per bonding family lowers the pooled held-out error from \SI{32.3}{\percent}
to \SI{21.7}{\percent}, though not significantly compound by compound (sign test $p = \num{0.44}$).
It is validated held out in five families, at \SIrange{7.4}{24.9}{\percent}; the best of these,
\ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}, rests mainly on one paper. Four further families fit their own papers but do not
transfer between members, because their members are different materials as made, and no prediction
is issued through them.

We issue \num{10} predictions with intervals, flag \num{15} candidates with the reason named for each,
and report \num{10} estimates as conditional on a measurement in their family. From crystal structure
alone we give $\kappa_L$ at \SI{300}{\kelvin} of \SI{1.39}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} for
\ce{TmPbAu} and \SI{5.46}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} for \ce{HfTeOs}, compounds for which we find no
published report. Each carries two separate error estimates: \SI{21.9}{\percent} for the model
(reference seed) and
\SI{33.0}{\percent} for the shared constant on families never measured.

The method estimates $\kappa_L$ at a stated temperature but does not predict its temperature
dependence, because the exponent is poorly determined (bootstrap range \numrange{0.45}{1.15}, against
\numrange{0.35}{0.55} for the magnitude). Scores depend on the single reference laboratory chosen per
compound, in a field whose laboratories disagree by a median factor of \num{1.33}; the intervals are
validated only inside the domain, and the method should not be used outside it.

A single synthesis and measurement of the cubic phase can falsify any issued prediction. Four kinds
of measurement would most improve the method: a first measurement in a family with no measured
member, such as \ce{Pb}--\ce{Au} or \ce{Te}--\ce{Os}; a second, bulk member from another laboratory in
each family that rests on one compound or one paper; wide-range measurements on dense specimens to
constrain the exponent; and two sound \ce{Ni}--\ce{Bi} measurements, free of the bipolar
contribution that rules out the existing \ce{YNiBi} curve, which would allow nine of the ten
conditional estimates to be issued. What holds these estimates back is a missing measured member
of their family, not a missing calculation.
