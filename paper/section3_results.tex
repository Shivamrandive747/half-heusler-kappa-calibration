\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

With the data and the tests fixed, the Results first measure the offset between calculation and
measurement (Section~\ref{sec:offset}) and test a two-parameter correction for it
(Section~\ref{sec:transferresults}). They then test the model that supplies a calculation where none
is published (Section~\ref{sec:mlrepro}), the model and the correction together against measurement
(Section~\ref{sec:blind}), and that result against further checks and a stronger baseline
(Section~\ref{sec:robust}). The remaining sections give the predictions the method issues and those
it declines.

\subsection{Published calculations systematically overestimate measured \texorpdfstring{$\kappa_L$}{kappa\_L}}
\label{sec:offset}

A correction is possible only if the offset between calculation and experiment is systematic. It
is. The calculation exceeds the measurement in \num{29} of the \num{34} compounds and in \num{10} of
the \num{12} bonding families, by a median factor of \num{1.69} (each compound's median ratio, then
the median over compounds; Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}(c)). The comparison takes every in-domain
half Heusler that has both a published full calculation and a laboratory measurement, each scored
against its single reference laboratory: \num{34} compounds and \num{429} calculation--measurement
pairs. The
five exceptions, \ce{HfSnPt}, \ce{TiSnPt}, \ce{ZrSnPt}, \ce{ErBiPd} and \ce{LaBiPd}, all belong to
the two families whose median calculation lies below measurement, \ce{Sn}--\ce{Pt} and
\ce{Bi}--\ce{Pd}.

The offset depends on temperature. The median ratio in each \SI{100}{\kelvin} bin falls from
\num{2.61} at \SI{200}{\kelvin} (\num{13} compounds) and \num{1.84} at \SI{300}{\kelvin} (\num{30})
to \num{0.98} at \SI{900}{\kelvin} (\num{11}; Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}(b)), so any correction needs a
temperature term. The bins do not hold the same compounds, and for nearly every pair the calculation
is carried in temperature as $1/T$ (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}), so the trend is that of the
measurements set against an assumed calculated slope. The offset
is also larger than the disagreement between independent calculations of the same compound, a median factor of \num{1.37} over \num{82} half Heuslers (Section~S9 of the Supplementary material), so it is not
the scatter of one code or one group. At a fixed temperature it shows no trend with how conductive
the compound is calculated to be: at \SI{300}{\kelvin} the ratio is uncorrelated with the calculated $\kappa_L$
(Spearman $\rho = \num{0.00}$ over the \num{30} compounds with a pair in the \SI{300}{\kelvin} bin). Section~\ref{sec:interpretation} returns to the
cause. What matters here is that the offset is systematic and can therefore be corrected.

\subsection{A two-parameter transfer function removes the median offset}
\label{sec:transferresults}

We now ask whether the two numbers of Eq.~\eqref{eq:transfer} remove the offset, whether a constant
per bonding family does better, and whether the correction holds beyond the data it was fitted on.

\paragraph{The shared constant} One constant and one exponent, shared by all compounds, remove the
median offset. Fitted on the \num{34} compounds of Section~\ref{sec:offset}, the shared form gives
$c=\num{0.42}$ and $p=\num{0.70}$ (bootstrap ranges in Section~\ref{sec:transfer}). Correcting each
compound with the shared constant refitted without its own chemistry cluster moves the median ratio
from \num{1.69} to \num{0.99} and the fraction of compounds within a factor of two of measurement
from \SI{68}{\percent} to \SI{88}{\percent} (Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}(a) and (c)).

A bias remains at room temperature: values quoted at \SI{300}{\kelvin} through the shared constant
tend to run low. The fit centres each compound across its own temperatures, and the fitted curve lies
above the bin median at every temperature (Fig.~\ref{fig:offset}(b)). At \SI{300}{\kelvin} it
implies \num{2.38} where the bin median is \num{1.84}, which gives the median compound in that bin
\num{0.77} of its measurement. Over all compound--bin
points the corrected median ratio is \num{0.90} (against \num{0.99} per compound, where each compound
is first reduced to its median over temperatures), the same median bias as in the blind test
(Section~\ref{sec:blind}).

\paragraph{One constant per bonding family}
\label{sec:familycal}
\label{sec:deployed}
Does a constant fitted to one bonding family transfer to a member it was not fitted on, and does it
then do better than the shared constant? In five of the nine families with two or more members the
median held-out error is under \SI{25}{\percent}; in the other four it is not. A family member (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is a
compound with both a usable reference measurement and a temperature-matched published calculation.
Each of the \num{12} families with a member carries its own constant
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:family}), tested leave-one-compound-out because an unmeasured target is predicted
the same way, from its measured family-mates.

Table~\ref{tab:famcal} and Fig.~\ref{fig:family}(a) give the families. The five validated families
have held-out errors from \SI{7.4}{\percent} (\ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}) to \SI{24.9}{\percent}
(\ce{Co}--\ce{Sb}). The family constant has the lower median error in three of them: it more than halves the error in \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt} and \ce{Sn}--\ce{Pt}, and lowers it from
\SI{28.7}{\percent} to \SI{17.3}{\percent} in \ce{Fe}--\ce{Sb}.
In \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb} and \ce{Co}--\ce{Sb} the shared constant does as well or better on the same
compounds; the pre-specified rule still gives every family with measured members its own constant,
so the issued \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb} values carry \num{0.47} (shared constant: \num{0.42}).

Three cautions apply. First, the data for \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt} come mainly from one paper, in which
three of the four members are measured at three temperatures each, one fewer than required
elsewhere, and were extracted by the language model, whose only independent check covers four
matched points and is a consistency check, not a validation (Section~S3 of the Supplementary
material). Second, the constant of that family is fitted without \ce{YSbPt}, the one member measured
in another laboratory, which the guard of Section~\ref{sec:transfer} sets aside as an outlier:
predicted from the other three, \ce{YSbPt} has an error of \SI{34.1}{\percent}, larger than with the
shared constant, and the family's \SI{7.4}{\percent} is the median over all four members. Third,
with three members in most families, a family's status can turn on a single compound;
\ce{Co}--\ce{Sb} lies just under the bar.

The other four families, \ce{Bi}--\ce{Pd}, \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sn}, \ce{Co}--\ce{Sn} and \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pd},
are \emph{calibrated, not validated}. Their constant fits each member's own paper
(\SIrange{2.8}{7.3}{\percent} in sample, against \SIrange{2.4}{13.9}{\percent} over all nine
families) but does not transfer from one member to another within the \SI{25}{\percent} bar. We
attribute this to differences between the members as made: a nanostructured sample set against a
dense one, or members measured in different laboratories (Section~\ref{sec:interpretation}). Their
held-out results and the reason in each family are in Section~S6 of the Supplementary material, and
no prediction is issued through them.

\paragraph{Pooled over the held-out compounds} Over all held-out compounds together, the family
constant lowers the median error but is not significantly better compound by compound. Thirty-one of
the \num{34} compounds belong to a family with at least one other measured member and can be
predicted held out. On these \num{31} the family constant gives a median error of
\SI{21.7}{\percent}, with \SI{93.5}{\percent} within a factor of two; the shared constant, refitted
without each held-out compound, gives \SI{32.3}{\percent} and \SI{83.9}{\percent}. The family
constant is the better of the two for \num{16} compounds, worse for \num{11} and tied for \num{4}
(two-sided sign test on the \num{27} untied, $p=\num{0.44}$). Figure~\ref{fig:family}(b) draws the
two errors of every compound and shows why the median falls although the counts are close: where
the family constant helps, the gain is often large; where it does worse, the loss is in most
cases smaller.
Section~S7 of the Supplementary material gives the same comparison over all \num{34} compounds,
which mixes in the three single-member fits, and the result for a capped family form.

Figure~\ref{fig:family}(c) draws held-out family fits as curves, for the compounds measured at
five or more temperatures spanning at least \SI{300}{\kelvin}. It shows the best of each
validated family that has such a compound: \ce{TiSnPt} (\SI{7.7}{\percent}),
\ce{LuNiSb}~\cite{ciesielski2020thermoelec} (\SI{8.0}{\percent}), \ce{VFeSb}
(\SI{11.4}{\percent}) and \ce{ZrCoSb} (\SI{13.4}{\percent}). In \ce{TiSnPt}, above about
\SI{400}{\kelvin} the uncapped family form raises the corrected curve above the calculation,
because the measurements lie there; Section~\ref{sec:interpretation} discusses why a measured
value can exceed the calculation. These are best cases. Over all \num{13} such compounds the
held-out error has a median of \SI{18.0}{\percent} and reaches \SI{41.1}{\percent}
(\ce{HfSnPt}), and Section~S5 of the Supplementary material draws every one of them.


\paragraph{The unseen-family test: the shared constant on families never measured}
\label{sec:unseenfamily}
For a family with no measured member only the shared constant applies, so its error there must be
known. Withholding one family at a time from the fit and predicting its members gives, over the
\num{34} compounds, a median error of \SI{33.0}{\percent} with \SI{79.4}{\percent} within a factor
of two and \SI{32.4}{\percent} within \SI{25}{\percent}; per-family medians range from
\SI{12.8}{\percent} (\ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb}) to \SI{116.8}{\percent} (\ce{Sb}--\ce{Pd}). Every
shared-constant estimate in this paper carries this calibration error.

\paragraph{The correction transfers to a separately computed set of calculations}
\label{sec:sdnnff}

All the calculations used so far come from density functional theory. Elemental-SDNNFF
\cite{rodriguez2023millionsca} lets us test the offset and the shared correction on
calculations made a different way. It
solves the same Boltzmann transport equation, but with interatomic forces predicted by a neural
network, and it predicts the phonon properties of \num{11866} Heusler structures. We used none of its
values to train or fit anything, so it is an independent check. We compare it three ways. First, it
agrees closely with the published first-principles calculations for the \num{18} in-domain compounds
both cover: the median ratio is \num{0.99} and the median difference \SI{18.8}{\percent} at
\SI{300}{\kelvin}. Second, it is a median \num{2.01} times higher than measurement for the \num{21}
compounds it shares with our validation set (median error \SI{101.4}{\percent}). A faithful copy of
the calculation therefore inherits the calculation's offset from experiment
(Section~\ref{sec:offset}). Third, our shared correction lowers the error on the same \num{21}
compounds from \SI{101.4}{\percent} to \SI{36.8}{\percent} (paired Wilcoxon $p=\num{0.0033}$). That
correction was fitted only on first-principles calculations, and refitted here without each
compound's chemistry cluster. It therefore works on calculations it was never fitted to.

Two qualifications apply. First, the agreement with first-principles calculations is not fully
independent: for \num{6} of the \num{18} compounds, the published median includes first-principles
values from the SDNNFF paper itself. Second, the model route of Section~\ref{sec:blind} gives
\SI{44.7}{\percent} on the same \num{21} compounds (reference seed). That is closer to measurement
than raw SDNNFF for \num{16} of the \num{21}, but not as close as corrected SDNNFF. The comparison
favours SDNNFF, which calculates each compound directly, whereas our model predicts each compound
with its whole chemistry withheld. Section~S7 lists the overlap between the two sets of calculations
and the temperature windows used to pair values.

Every test in this subsection started from a calculation, because the correction acts on one. Where
none is published, one has to be supplied first.

\subsection{The model reproduces published calculations}
\label{sec:mlrepro}

A model can supply the calculation where none is published. Tested on the compounds that have one,
it reproduces them to a median error of \SI{21.9}{\percent} (reference seed). The score is against
the published full calculations of the \num{230} $\mathrm{VEC}=18$ half Heuslers, each predicted
with its chemistry cluster withheld from training (\num{27} clusters); \SI{88.3}{\percent}
lie within a factor of two, and the median ratio is \num{0.87} (Fig.~\ref{fig:modelblind}(a)). The
result covers only the step from structure to calculation. The blind test below already
includes this step, so the two errors should not be compounded.

\paragraph{What the model has learned}
\label{sec:shap}

To see which descriptors the model relies on, we decompose the reference-seed model, fitted on all
\num{681} training rows, with \textsc{shap} (SHapley Additive exPlanations)~\cite{lundberg2017a};
Section~S4 of the Supplementary material gives the attribution in full. The full-Heusler flag
contributes most, followed by size and bond length: a larger X-site radius and longer bonds lower
the prediction. The pattern is consistent with phonon physics, which does not show that the model
has learned that physics. Two limits matter most: the attribution explains the model of the
\emph{calculation}, not of a measurement, which also depends on the specimen; and correlated
descriptors share attribution, so the ranking describes groups of descriptors more reliably than
individual ones.

The correction and the model have now each been tested alone, the correction on published
calculations and the model against them. The next test applies both and compares the result with measurement.

\subsection{From crystal structure to measurement: the held-out test against experiment}
\label{sec:blind}

The central test of the paper asks how close the model route, model plus shared transfer function,
comes to measurement for a compound whose chemistry was withheld from every fitted step. We score
the \num{39} in-domain half Heuslers, each against its single reference laboratory with its chemistry cluster removed from both the model's training and the fit of the shared constant.

The model route has a median error of \SI{33.1}{\percent} (bootstrap 95\,\% interval
\SIrange{23.6}{38.9}{\percent}) and places \SI{87.2}{\percent} of compounds within a factor of two
of measurement, both medians over five model seeds (Table~\ref{tab:accuracy};
Fig.~\ref{fig:modelblind}(b) and (c)). Across seeds the median error runs from \SI{24.6}{\percent}
to \SI{35.4}{\percent} and the within-$2\times$ fraction from \SI{87.2}{\percent} to
\SI{89.7}{\percent}; the reference seed, at which the per-compound analyses below are quoted, gives
\SI{31.7}{\percent}, slightly better than the seed median. Without the domain screens, over all
\num{50} measured half Heuslers, the same protocol gives \SI{37.4}{\percent} (seed range
\SIrange{33.6}{38.9}{\percent}), against \SI{48.2}{\percent} for the uncorrected model (medians over
seeds). The correction moves the median ratio of predicted to measured conductivity from \num{1.40}
to \num{0.90}. Being a multiplier, it cannot remove the compression visible in
Fig.~\ref{fig:modelblind}(b): the lowest-$\kappa_L$ compounds tend to be over-predicted and the
highest under-predicted.

\paragraph{Against the null baselines}
\label{sec:vsnulls}
A route is useful only if it beats a prediction that knows nothing about the compound, and here the
answer depends on how the baselines are fitted. Both null baselines are refitted with the same
chemistry held out and scored against the same references. Fitted to the individual measurements,
as first specified, they have median errors of \SI{46.7}{\percent} and \SI{45.6}{\percent}
(Table~\ref{tab:accuracy}), and the route is closer to measurement than each null for
\SI{71.8}{\percent} of compounds, by a median \num{10.3}
percentage points for the constant null (95\,\% interval \numrange{3.8}{22.9}) and \num{11.2} for
the power law (\numrange{3.5}{22.9}). A paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives $p=\num{0.004}$
against each null at the median over seeds, and $p=\num{0.007}$ after a Holm correction for the two
comparisons; on the least favourable seed the Holm-adjusted value is \num{0.056}.

Fitted compound by compound, as the constants of the route are (Section~\ref{sec:nulls}), the
baselines come much closer. The constant then has a median error of \SI{35.9}{\percent}, with
\SI{76.9}{\percent} of compounds within a factor of two, against \SI{33.1}{\percent} and
\SI{87.2}{\percent} for the route. The route is the closer of the two for \SI{56.4}{\percent} of
compounds, and the median paired difference of \num{6.1} percentage points has a 95\,\% interval
that runs from \num{18.3} in favour of the route to \num{4.3} against it; the Wilcoxon test gives
$p=\num{0.24}$ at the median over seeds. The power law has a median error of \SI{42.2}{\percent}
(\SI{74.4}{\percent} within a factor of two); the route is closer for \SI{66.7}{\percent} of
compounds, by a median \num{10.3} points (\numrange{0.8}{19.8}), with $p=\num{0.026}$, or
\num{0.052} after the Holm adjustment. On median percentage error the route is therefore not shown
to beat a constant fitted in the same way. It does place more compounds within a factor of two, a
difference we have not tested.

Two stricter tests weaken the evidence for the first fit as well. Compounds of one chemistry cluster
share a model fold and often a laboratory. With each of the \num{18} clusters counted once and the
baselines fitted to the measurements, the Wilcoxon test still gives $p<0.05$ on every seed before
the Holm adjustment (largest \num{0.048}), but the sign test does not, so the cluster-level evidence
is marginal; with the baselines fitted per compound it is absent (largest $p$ of \num{1.00} for the
constant and \num{0.26} for the power law). Over all \num{50} measured compounds the route still
improves on both nulls fitted to the measurements, more weakly (paired Wilcoxon $p=\num{0.035}$ and \num{0.033} at the reference seed, unadjusted; Section~S7). Fitted to the measurements, the nulls place almost as many compounds within a factor of two as the
route (\SI{84.6}{\percent}) and lose on the typical error; fitted per compound, they come close on the
typical error and place fewer within a factor of two. Neither fit shows the route clearly ahead once
each chemistry cluster is counted once.

\paragraph{The errors set against the reproducibility of the field}
The remaining error can be set beside the spread between two sources that report the same compound.
Figure~\ref{fig:modelblind}(c) places both on one multiplicative scale, the factor
$10^{|\log_{10} r|}$ by which two values differ, whichever is the larger. Two laboratories
measuring the same in-domain half Heusler differ by a median factor of \num{1.36} (\num{17} compounds), and two
independent calculations by \num{1.37}. Uncorrected, published calculations sit outside this range
(\num{1.69}), as do the model's uncorrected values (\num{1.48}) and the two null baselines (\num{1.53} and \num{1.56}; \num{1.49} and \num{1.54} when fitted per compound). With the shared constant, the published calculations (\num{1.37}) and
the model route (\num{1.35}) come down to the size of the laboratory spread, and family constants go
slightly below it (\num{1.28}). The comparison is one of size only: the spread rows compare two
sources with each other, the other rows compare a prediction with one chosen reference, and the
scored compound sets differ. Within that limit, the remaining error is of the order of the
disagreement between measurements. We next ask whether further checks and a stronger baseline change this result.


\subsection{Robustness and comparisons}
\label{sec:robust}

\paragraph{Further checks}
\label{sec:failure}
Further checks support the result; Section~S7 gives each in full, including a correction without
the temperature term and the rank correlation between predicted and measured conductivity. The
eleven compounds the screens exclude behave as a separate population, with a median error of
\SI{86.4}{\percent} against \SI{31.7}{\percent} inside the domain at the reference seed (open
squares in Fig.~\ref{fig:modelblind}(b)). Published semi-empirical estimates
(Slack~\cite{slack1979the} and Debye--Callaway~\cite{callaway1959model}) do far worse, at
\SI{91.6}{\percent} on the \num{22} in-domain compounds that have one.

\paragraph{Where the error falls, and a family-informed baseline}
\label{sec:familynull}
The error is uneven across bonding families. Among families with three or more blind-test
compounds, on the model route at the reference seed, the worst are
\ce{Co}--\ce{Sn} (\SI{59.4}{\percent}) and \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sn} (\SI{55.4}{\percent}), three compounds
each, and the best is \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb} (\SI{13.7}{\percent}, eight compounds). These counts are of
blind-test compounds, which include measured compounds without a published calculation, so they can
exceed the members of Table~\ref{tab:famcal}; the grouping is descriptive, since the unit held out is the chemistry cluster. A stronger baseline therefore fits a power law to
the measured $\kappa(T)$ of the held-out compound's own family, with no calculation. It is defined
for \num{34} of the \num{39} in-domain compounds (the five exceptions are listed in Section~S7; not
the same \num{34} as the published route). There it gives \SI{25.4}{\percent} against
\SI{31.6}{\percent} for the model route at the reference seed, a difference that is not significant
(paired Wilcoxon $p=\num{0.99}$); Section~\ref{sec:routes} draws the consequence for choosing a
route. These checks leave the main result unchanged, with one
qualification: where a family has been measured, a power law through its own measurements does as
well as the model route. We turn to prediction.

\subsection{Ten predictions for unmeasured compounds}
\label{sec:screening}
\label{sec:issued}

This section states which unmeasured half Heuslers can be given a value, and what supports each.
The lowest median held-out error was that of a published calculation corrected with its family constant
(Section~\ref{sec:familycal}), so we apply that route to half Heuslers that have a published
transport calculation but no measurement. A candidate
is issued only if it meets every condition below:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Inside the domain of applicability} (Section~\ref{sec:domain}), with no radioactive
        element and none of \ce{Ce}, \ce{Sm}, \ce{Eu} or \ce{Yb}.
  \item \textbf{A bonding family whose constant has been checked}: \emph{validated} (held-out error
        under \SI{25}{\percent}) or \emph{anchored} on a single member whose in-sample fit is under
        the same bar, an anchored prediction carrying that label (Table~\ref{tab:famcal}).
  \item \textbf{A full transport calculation}, independent calculations agreeing within a factor of
        two at the quoted temperature.
  \item \textbf{Inside, or only marginally outside, the family's measured range.} A value beyond the
        family's measured span by less than a factor of \num{1.33}, the median disagreement between
        laboratories over all pairs of measurements (\num{1.36} per compound), is issued with a
        \emph{marginal} label; a value further outside is flagged.
        An anchored family has a single member and so defines no span; this condition does not apply
        to it.
\end{enumerate}

Ten compounds meet every condition (Table~\ref{tab:predictions}, Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions}). Three
lie inside the span of a validated family, \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt} or \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb}, each differing from
the measured members only on the rare-earth site. Three are marginal extrapolations in
\ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}; for the six measured compounds lying this close outside their family-mates' span,
the held-out error showed no detectable difference from that of the \num{19} inside it, a comparison
too small to show that the two groups are alike (Section~S7). Four are issued through the
anchored families \ce{Co}--\ce{Bi} and \ce{Ni}--\ce{Pb}, three of them at \SI{500}{\kelvin}, the
temperature of their only uncontested calculation.

The intervals may be too narrow where a prediction rests on one calculation. They are the conformal
intervals of Section~\ref{sec:intervals} and vary little across the five seeds (Section~S7). The
calculations being calibrated come from published high-throughput
studies~\cite{li2025highthroug,ohnishi2026database,miyazaki2021machine,carrete2014finding,trans2022lattice},
most from one~\cite{miyazaki2021machine}, and seven of the ten rest on a single calculation at the
quoted temperature. Independent calculations of the same half Heusler disagree by a median factor of
\num{1.37}, and the interval, built from the held-out errors of the model route, does not add
the spread among published calculations, so a single-calculation interval may understate the
uncertainty. Nine of the ten have an uncontested calculation at only one temperature (\ce{HfCoBi}
has two), so no other temperature is quoted.

\ce{GdNiSb} also carries a structural caveat the databases do not record: it has been reported in a
high-temperature \ce{AlB2}-type hexagonal form beside the low-temperature \ce{MgAgAs}-type cubic
one~\cite{skolozdra1997magnetic}, and the prediction is for the cubic phase. Not every candidate
with a corrected value meets the four conditions; we turn to those that do not.

\subsection{Flagged compounds and the measurements that would resolve them}
\label{sec:refusals}

Fifteen compounds carry a corrected value but are declined (\emph{flagged}); Section~S11 of the
Supplementary material tabulates each with the condition it fails and draws every status on one axis.
Most belong to a family calibrated on its papers but not validated held out: nine to
\ce{Bi}--\ce{Pd} and two to \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pd}. \ce{LaSbPt} and \ce{ScSbPt} are clear extrapolations
in a validated family: \ce{LaSbPt} lies a factor of \num{2.00} below the measured \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}
floor and \ce{ScSbPt} \num{1.97} above its ceiling. The last two belong to \ce{Sb}--\ce{Ir}, whose
single measured member its own constant does not reproduce within the bar. The range criterion is
deliberately conservative: the two held-out compounds that lay further outside their family's span
were predicted to a median of \SI{18.4}{\percent}, too few cases to support issuing clear
extrapolations.

Ten further estimates, in \ce{Ni}--\ce{Bi}, are conditional, because the family cannot be
calibrated. Its only measured member, \ce{YNiBi}, has a thermal conductivity with a significant
bipolar contribution, as its authors report~\cite{li2015synthesis}, and the lattice term we extract
from it rises with temperature. On the shared constant the ten lie at
\SIrange{2.21}{4.95}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} at \SI{300}{\kelvin}; Section~S12 of the
Supplementary material lists them with the two measurements that would allow nine of them to be
issued. Sections~S13 to~S15 give the status of \ce{YNiBi} and the measurements that would release or
strengthen the other flagged, anchored and structure-only values. One of the ten, \ce{SmNiBi}, also carries a caveat, because samarium's valence can vary
between compounds; it is counted as conditional, not among the fifteen flagged. Across the three
statuses the conformal interval has the same relative width; what changes is the evidence behind the
value, not its stated precision.

For one family a measurement of the cubic phase may not be possible at all. The cobalt arsenides
\ce{XCoAs} (X = Ti, Zr, Hf) have published calculations but no measured family member, and the two
we found reported as synthesised, \ce{ZrCoAs} and \ce{HfCoAs}, crystallise in the orthorhombic
\ce{Co2Si} type~\cite{kleinke1998crystal}. We therefore carry none of the three as a candidate, so
none has a status; the polymorph screen alone would have caught only \ce{HfCoAs} (Section~S11).

Every candidate value so far starts from a published calculation. What can be said for a compound that has
none?

\subsection{Two predictions from crystal structure alone}
\label{sec:structonly}

A value can still be given from crystal structure alone (Section~\ref{sec:structureonly}), but it is
less certain than an issued prediction. Each carries two separately tested errors, stated side by
side and not combined: \SI{21.9}{\percent} for the model step (reference seed;
Section~\ref{sec:mlrepro}) and \SI{33.0}{\percent} for the shared constant on a family never
measured (Section~\ref{sec:unseenfamily}). No conformal interval is given, because its coverage is
not validated for a family never measured. Two compounds are graded strong on the evidence for their
model step; Fig.~\ref{fig:predictions} (bottom) gives both values for each.

For \ce{TmPbAu} (\ce{Pb}--\ce{Au} family; OQMD cubic cell of the lowest-energy ordering,
$a=\SI{6.782}{\angstrom}$), the six compounds of the family with published calculations are reproduced held
out to between \SI{1}{\percent} and \SI{13}{\percent} (median \SI{8.6}{\percent}), and the seeds
agree within a factor of \num{1.11}. The model gives \SI{3.31}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} and the
shared constant ($c=\num{0.42}$) \SI{1.39}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} at \SI{300}{\kelvin}. A family neighbour, \ce{TbPbAu}, has been grown as single
crystals in the cubic half-Heusler structure~\cite{agarwal2026thermodyna}.

For \ce{HfTeOs} (\ce{Te}--\ce{Os} family; AFLOW cubic cell of the lowest-energy ordering,
$a=\SI{6.33}{\angstrom}$), the strong grade rests on one member, \ce{TiTeOs}, reproduced to
\SI{0.5}{\percent} held out; the seeds spread by a factor of \num{1.23}. The model gives
\SI{12.99}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin} and the shared constant
\SI{5.46}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}. As a cross-check, the SDNNFF value for its chemical twin
\ce{ZrTeOs} (Zr in place of Hf)~\cite{rodriguez2023millionsca}, carried to hafnium by the twin ratio of the Extended
Methods, gives \SI{14.06}{\watt\per\meter\per\kelvin}; the model value is \num{0.92} times this. The
twin ratio was validated on calculations (median error \SI{12.2}{\percent}), not on a
machine-learned input, so that figure does not carry over to this check.

Section~S16 of the Supplementary material reports the other compounds the search found: further
compounds with no published $\kappa_L$, a group with only a machine-learned value from a published
screen, and two compounds in calibrated families that are flagged. The Discussion
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) takes up what these results mean physically, which route to use and how
far the routes reach.
