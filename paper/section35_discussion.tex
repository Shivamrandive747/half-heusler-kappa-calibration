\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The Results show that published calculations exceed measurement by an offset that is systematic
and shrinks as temperature rises, and that a two-parameter transfer function removes the median
offset for compounds held out of its fit. With a model supplying the calculation, the model route
disagrees with measurement by an amount similar in size to the spread between two laboratories
measuring the same compound. None of this says what the offset is. We take that question first,
then ask which route to use for a given compound and how far the routes reach.

\subsection{What the offset and the constants mean}
\label{sec:interpretation}

What causes the offset, and do the fitted constants have a physical meaning? Several effects could
contribute to the offset, and each would leave a different signature (Table~\ref{tab:causes}). The
first is the specimen. A real specimen scatters phonons at defects that the calculation of a
perfect crystal lacks: interstitials and antisites, imaged directly in \ce{TiNiSn}-based
alloys~\cite{kim2017direct}, and grain boundaries~\cite{schrade2017the}. In calculations for
\num{54} half-Heusler alloys, alloy and grain-boundary scattering significantly reduce the
intrinsic conductivity~\cite{berland2019thermoelec}. Such scattering is nearly independent of
temperature, so it matters less as Umklapp scattering strengthens. The second is four-phonon
scattering, which most calculations omit. It lowers the true conductivity, so omitting it biases
three-phonon calculations high~\cite{feng2017fourphonon}; a full treatment of quartic
anharmonicity also shifts phonon frequencies, however, and its net effect differs between
structure types~\cite{xia2020highthroug}. The third is the set-up of the calculation: the
exchange--correlation functional and force-constant cut-offs move a calculation either way. The
fourth lies on the measurement side. An electronic or bipolar term left in $\kappa_L$ by the
Lorenz subtraction raises the measured value~\cite{thesberg2017on,li2015synthesis}, and the
bipolar part grows with temperature as more carriers are excited across the band gap.

Read against Table~\ref{tab:causes}, the three observations of Section~\ref{sec:offset} narrow the
possibilities without settling them. The offset shrinks with temperature, which fits specimen
scattering or a residual electronic term better than missing four-phonon scattering, whose effect
would grow. For scale, if the whole \SI{300}{\kelvin} offset were extrinsic scattering added by
Matthiessen's rule,
$\kappa_L^{\mathrm{exp}}/\kappa_L^{\mathrm{BTE}} = 1/(1+\tau_{\mathrm{int}}/\tau_{\mathrm{ext}})$ in a
grey model, the bin median of \num{1.84} would require an extrinsic scattering rate \num{0.84} times
the intrinsic one. The offset also exceeds the spread between independent calculations, so
differences between codes and settings are not enough to explain it. And at \SI{300}{\kelvin} it
shows no trend with the calculated conductivity: a single boundary mean free path common to every
specimen would suppress the most conductive compounds, whose intrinsic mean free paths are
longest, more strongly. That argues against one common grain size as the whole explanation; defect
populations that differ from specimen to specimen, or errors in the calculations themselves,
remain possible.

The same caution applies to the two constants. Phenomenological temperature dependences
have been fitted to, or tested against, measured half-Heusler curves
before~\cite{geng2014lattice,petersen2015critical}; here we fit instead the \emph{ratio} of
measurement to calculation. The functional form follows that earlier work; what is new here is the
corpus, the class-wide magnitude and its held-out validation. Because nearly every pair carries
the assumed $T^{-1}$ calculated dependence (Section~\ref{sec:transfer}; the one multi-temperature
calculation has a log--log slope of \num{-1.02}), $p$ is in effect the measured slope relative to
it: $p=\num{0.70}$ implies a corrected $\kappa_L \propto T^{-0.30}$.

The correction is empirical, not a boundary-scattering term. In a relaxation-time description of
the conductivity~\cite{callaway1959model}, any temperature-independent extrinsic scattering
(boundaries, point defects or static disorder) added to three-phonon Umklapp scattering caps the
logarithmic slope of the suppression at $1-c$ (\num{0.58} at $c=\num{0.42}$), for a grey or a
spectral model; this bound is our own derivation (Section~S1 of the Supplementary material). The
fitted $p$ exceeds that cap, although its bootstrap range straddles it, and four-phonon scattering
would lower the slope. A measured lattice conductivity above the three-phonon value of the perfect
crystal, as some high-temperature curves show, more likely retains an electronic or bipolar term
that the single-parabolic-band Lorenz number does not remove, or high-temperature measurement
error: the Lorenz number of a multiband or bipolar semiconductor can depart from the single-band
value by a factor of two or more~\cite{thesberg2017on}. Part of the rise of the
measured-to-calculated ratio with temperature, and of the value of $p$, may therefore reflect the
separation of lattice and electronic conduction rather than phonon scattering, and we attach no
mechanism to the parameters.

A family constant describes the specimens behind it, and it may also absorb measurement effects.
The \ce{Bi}--\ce{Pd} constant is close to one, and its rare-earth members are the narrow-gap
compounds for which the electronic subtraction is least secure (Section~\ref{sec:domain}); part of
it may reflect an electronic term left in the measured values rather than better crystals. Several
families rest on a single kind of specimen (the publications behind each family are counted in
Section~S5 of the Supplementary material). All three \ce{Sn}--\ce{Pt} references come from one
paper, as do three of the four in \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pt}, four of the seven in \ce{Ni}--\ce{Sb} and two
of the three in each of \ce{Bi}--\ce{Pd} and \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pd}; there the held-out test partly
measures one paper's internal consistency rather than transfer between laboratories.

The specimens behind the constants also differ in kind. Two \ce{Sb}--\ce{Pd} references
are annealed arc-melted ingots whose authors attribute the near-linear low-temperature
conductivity to additional scattering at grain boundaries, lattice defects and
disorder~\cite{mukhopadhyay2018multifunct}. The two \ce{Co}--\ce{Sn} members were measured on very
different specimens, \ce{NbCoSn} on a near-fully dense pellet~\cite{thiem2025thermoelec} and
\ce{TaCoSn} on a heavily ball-milled nanocrystalline, cobalt-rich specimen that its authors
suggest contains cobalt interstitials~\cite{li2019ntype}; a constant fitted on one does not
transfer to the other (Section~S6). An anchored family inherits its single member's specimen.
\ce{ZrNiPb} was measured only on a ball-milled specimen~\cite{mao2017thermoelec}, so values issued
through \ce{Ni}--\ce{Pb} describe a milled specimen. The \ce{ZrCoBi} reference is a
\SI{98.1}{\percent} dense pellet~\cite{zhao2018synthesis}, whereas the only other laboratory to
measure it reports a value \num{1.7} times lower near \SI{300}{\kelvin} on a far more heavily
milled specimen~\cite{zhu2018discovery}. The \ce{Co}--\ce{Bi} predictions therefore carry a wider
uncertainty than their conformal interval.

\subsection{Which route to use}
\label{sec:routes}

For estimating the measured $\kappa_L$ of an unmeasured half Heusler, the results suggest a simple
order of preference among the routes, set by what is known about the compound. Where a
full transport calculation has been published and the compound's bonding family has been
validated, the family constant gives the lowest median error of the routes tested: over the
\num{31} compounds with a measured family-mate it gives \SI{21.7}{\percent} held out, against
\SI{32.3}{\percent} for the shared constant on the same compounds, a difference that is not
significant compound by compound (Section~\ref{sec:familycal}). Where a calculation exists but
the family has no measured member, only the shared constant applies, with the \SI{33.0}{\percent}
median error measured on families withheld from the fit. Where no calculation exists, the
machine-learning model supplies one. For a new X-site substitution in a family with measured
members, model and shared constant together are tested end to end at \SI{33.1}{\percent} inside the
domain and \SI{37.4}{\percent} over all measured compounds (Section~\ref{sec:blind}); in a family never measured, the value carries two separate errors,
\SI{21.9}{\percent} for the model step (reference seed) and \SI{33.0}{\percent} for the shared
constant.

Where a family has already been measured, a power law fitted to its members' measured $\kappa(T)$
does as well as the model route (\SI{25.4}{\percent} against \SI{31.6}{\percent} for the model
route at the reference seed; Section~\ref{sec:familynull}), but it cannot order compounds within a
family and says nothing where no member is measured. The method therefore adds most where
measurements are sparse.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The method estimates $\kappa_L$ at a stated temperature. It is not designed to reproduce the shape of
$\kappa_L(T)$: the training labels lie mostly at \SI{300}{\kelvin} and one exponent serves every
compound. Its accuracy is best read against the spread between laboratories (Section~\ref{sec:blind}),
since each compound is scored against one reference laboratory chosen by a fixed rule. Because
compounds share chemistry, the main tests hold out whole chemistry clusters; compounds that also
share a laboratory are discussed in Section~S17. Family constants
apply within families that have measured members, and other families receive the shared constant.
Each issued value inherits the accuracy of the calculation it starts from, and its source is listed so
that it can be updated as new calculations appear. Structure-only values are conditional on the compound forming in the cubic phase. Several alternatives, among them a
two-stage model, a magnitude-dependent calibration term and training on experimental or doped values,
were tested and not adopted because they did not improve held-out accuracy. Section~S17 gives the
quantitative detail behind each of these points.

Several of these limits are set by the measurements available; the Conclusions end with the
measurements that would lift them.
